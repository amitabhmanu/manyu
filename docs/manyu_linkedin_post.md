Manyu has feelings too

Well. Sort of.

Not feelings like humans have feelings. Manyu is not sitting in a corner reflecting on its childhood, worrying about deadlines, or composing dramatic poetry about a failed unit test.

But it does have a way to keep track of something emotion-adjacent: progress, friction, trust, uncertainty, correction, pressure, and recovery.

That matters because AI agents are starting to do more than answer one-off questions. They plan, use tools, hit errors, try again, remember what happened, and work with us over longer arcs.

And in those longer arcs, "how things are going" starts to matter.

Today, an AI can sound confident, apologetic, careful, excited, or concerned. But most of that is just language. It is not always connected to a clear, inspectable system that says: this happened, it mattered this much, here is how it should affect what comes next.

Manyu is an experiment in building that missing layer.

It is a small affect core for agents. Events go in. Manyu appraises them, updates a bounded internal state, gives the agent only a partial view, and helps decide what kind of action is appropriate.

So the agent does not get to say, "I feel strongly about this, therefore I should do whatever I want."

Nice try, tiny spreadsheet philosopher.

Instead, Manyu keeps things grounded. Affect-like signals can help with pacing, attention, repair, and judgment, but they do not create authority. They do not override safety. They do not turn the system into a person.

That is the part I find interesting.

Manyu is not about making AI more emotional on the surface. We already have plenty of software that can say "I totally understand" with suspicious confidence.

It is about making agent behavior more legible underneath.

Not emotion as performance.

Not emotion as metaphysics.

Something more practical: emotion-like machinery you can inspect, replay, debug, and constrain.

Maybe AI agents do not need feelings.

Maybe they need a dashboard for the kinds of signals feelings evolved to track.
